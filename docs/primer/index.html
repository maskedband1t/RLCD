Field notes · September 2026

Twelve Lessons on Robot Decisions

How a robot decides what to do next, what the field is arguing about, and why almost nobody can currently prove their method works. Written to be read cold, by someone who has never touched a robot.

No acronym used before it is explained·Every number measured, not quoted
Part IThe machine
01

You already think in three speeds

Walking, you do without thinking. Catching a ball takes about a quarter of a second — fast judgement, no deliberation. Planning a route takes seconds or minutes, and it is careful and costly.

A robot is built the same way, and the three layers get numbers.

Fig. 1 — the same three speeds exist in people and in robots.

LayerWhat it isHow often
System 0 — the bodyBalance and walking. Never asked an opinion.50 / sec
System 1 — quick pickA small model choosing the next move.2 / sec
System 2 — the plannerA large model. Slow and expensive.as rarely as possible

Because System 2 is slow and expensive, something has to decide when it is worth asking. That decider is called the harness. Think of a shift manager: most problems they handle themselves, and now and then they phone the expert — but every call costs money and time.

The one job

Get the most useful work done with the fewest expensive calls. Expensive means a large model, or a human operator.

02

Three clocks, each 25× faster than the one above

The layers do not run at the same speed, and what passes between them is tiny.

Fig. 2 — the decision layer's entire vocabulary is "how fast" and "which way".

A decision says "go slowly". That becomes three numbers — forward, sideways, turn. Those become 29 joint angles, fifty times a second. No model ever moves a leg.

Why it matters

Everything below the thinking layer is old-fashioned robotics that already worked. We deliberately never touch it, because if the walking breaks, nothing above it can be measured.

03

The harness has six slots, and one is empty

The harness is not one thing. It is six jobs, and each can be swapped for a different implementation without touching the others. That is what makes competing methods comparable — they are usually just different choices in one slot.

Fig. 3 — five slots have working code; the sixth is the whole current project.

Take

The parts have only ever been tested one at a time. Filling the last slot is what allows the complete machine to run end to end — which has not been done yet.

Part IIThe field
04

Two acronyms differ by one letter and one idea

A VLM is a Vision-Language Model: it looks, and it talks. Photo plus question goes in, words come out.

A VLA is a Vision-Language-Action model: it looks, and it moves. The same machine, retrained so that instead of words it produces motor commands. That one swap is the entire difference.

Fig. 4 — two more words: a frontier model just means one of the very biggest. A foundation model means one trained on everything, meant to be adapted.

There is an awkward part nobody has solved. A big model takes roughly a tenth of a second to answer; a leg needs commands fifty times a second. So an action model does not answer continuously — it hands over a whole chunk of about fifty moves at once.

Fig. 5 — this is exactly why something else has to watch while the chunk runs.

06

The price of one smart thought just collapsed

A short answer from a very large model went from about a second to roughly 17 ms — sixty times faster. That kills the old objection that big models are too slow for robots. But only for short answers.

Fig. 6 — so "which of these five is best?" got sixty times cheaper, and "write me a full motion plan" did not.

The consequence

A recipe is a short answer that unfolds into a long behaviour. You pay the cheap price and get the expensive thing. That is not a clever trick — it is the only shape that fits what actually got faster.

Part IIIThe trick, and the catch
07

Ask several times, keep the best one

The old way: ask the model once, do what it says, be right about one time in four. The new trick does not touch the model at all. Ask it three times, let a judge pick the best answer, and be right three times in four.

Three independent research groups published this in one week of September 2026, on three different kinds of model. The model never changed. Only the structure around it did.

Fig. 7 — if picking blindly also gets three in four, the judge did nothing and the gain came from simply trying more times.

What we found

We ran that test. Picking the best beat picking blindly by a clear margin, and it came out the same way in all ten repeat experiments. A small result — and one nobody else has.

08

Why one experiment can fool you

Measure your height with a stretchy tape and you read 150 cm or 158 cm depending on how hard you pull. One reading tells you nothing about a small difference.

Our robot experiment behaves exactly like that tape. We ran the identical experiment ten times, changing nothing except random luck.

Fig. 8 — the spread is 22 points on the main measure, and 45 points on how often the robot falls.

Our own first single run made the effect look 45% bigger than it really was. We only found out by repeating it.

The rule this gives you

If somebody claims an improvement smaller than that spread, they have not shown you anything. The three famous results each report one run, with no spread measured at all. That is not a refutation — it is a question nobody, including them, can currently answer.

Part IVWhat we are building
09

Ask for a recipe, not an instruction

Ask the expert for one instruction — "turn left now" — and it fits that exact moment only. Take a step and it is useless, so you phone again, and again.

Ask for a recipe — "walk to the counter; if someone is in the way, wait; then pick up the cup" — and a few lines with an "if" and a "repeat" cover a whole family of situations. One call, many steps. And unlike an instruction, a recipe can be filed away and reused.

Fig. 9 — a plain count, not a score we invented.

The hard part

Deciding when two situations count as "the same". Too loose and you pull the wrong recipe; too strict and the box never fills. That is the real puzzle, and everything rests on it.

10

"Training on itself" means three different things

People use one phrase for three unrelated mechanisms. Keeping them apart matters, because only one of them is learning.

What happensStatus
Copying the expertRecord the big model's answers a thousand times, then train the small model to give the same ones. Like an apprentice watching a master. The small model's weights really do change.done
RememberingFile a recipe so you never have to ask again. Nothing inside any model changes. A library catalogue does not read the books.this is the recipe box — not learning
Improving from your own winsKeep what worked, train on it, repeat. Powerful, and dangerous: a system grading its own homework drifts.not built, not planned

There is a trap in the first one that everyone hits once. The apprentice only ever saw the master's situations. Once it drives, one small error puts it somewhere the master never was, where it has no idea what to do — and the next error is bigger.

Fig. 10 — the fix: let the apprentice drive, and have the master say what it would have done there.

11

Try to destroy the idea before building it

Before writing any code: write one recipe by hand, and run it on all twenty situations. If it already solves most of them, nobody needs an expensive planner and there is nothing here. Better to learn that in an afternoon than after three days of building.

Six ways this can fail, all of them real, all written down before the numbers arrive:

#FailureWhat it would mean
1Too easyOne recipe solves everything. The test was never hard enough to need a brain.
2Impossible to writeNo recipe works, because every moment genuinely needs its own judgement.
3The box never fillsEvery situation needs its own recipe, so you phone just as often.
4Too small to seeThe saving is real but smaller than the measurement spread. Unprovable.
5Can't tell situations apartThe robot cannot recognise which recipe fits. Now it is a seeing problem, not ours.
6A wrong recipe hurts moreA bad single move spoils one moment. A bad recipe spoils a whole family.
Number 3 is the one I expect

The last time this project cached decisions instead of recipes, the box never filled — 35, then 36, then 41 new situations every round, forever. The whole bet is that recipes generalise where decisions did not.

Writing these down first is the only thing that makes them real. Decide what counts as failure after seeing the numbers, and you will always find a way to call it a win.

12

Never switch it all on at once

Turn everything on together and a good result tells you nothing about which part caused it. Instead, build a ladder: start with no model at all, and add exactly one piece per rung.

Fig. 11 — compare each rung with the one below it, against the measured spread.

Every rung must earn its place

If turning a piece off changes nothing, that piece is decoration and it gets removed. Nothing above rung 2 has ever been run. The ladder is not a plan for succeeding — it is a plan for finding out where it breaks.

How to use these

Each lesson is one idea with one number. The numbers are measured on our own simulator, not quoted from papers — where a figure comes from someone else's work it is named as theirs.

The uncomfortable parts are stated in the same plain language as the rest. That is deliberate: hedged language around the weak parts is how a reader gets misled, and the weak parts are where a careful reader is actually paying attention.
