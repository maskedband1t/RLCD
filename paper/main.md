## Abstract
*Draft v5, 2026-09-22 01:04 PDT. v4 (2026-09-20) is kept below as history. Every number is a notebook entry; all are re-checked at submission.*

Robot fleets that are not yet autonomous run on a boundary: when does the policy act, and when does a person? We measure what a typed, calibrated judgment interface (RLCD-trained System One models, of which Jev is the first) does at that boundary, against rule programs, an oracle, an open 27B model through the same readout, and a distilled head the fleet owns, in three simulated instruments with fixed controllers and a person in the workspace (a sorting cell, a small biped's room, a human-sized humanoid's fetch room) and one real teleoperation dataset, with predictions dated before every run (more than 115 experiments, 36 logged method errors). Four findings. (1) Where no rule was written, the judge handles the situation and the rule program does not (biped 29 of 30 against 1; the humanoid's two note situations 10 of 10 against 0); where the rules were written, the rules win (95 % against 82 %; 39 against 33 of 40). Rewritten by their author after seeing the unwritten bank, the rules match or beat the judge within fifteen minutes per body, so the judge's value is the interval before a rule exists, not accuracy after. (2) What fills that interval is a number: the judge's probability stays within .02 of its hit rate on the states its own actions create while the open 27B's runs .135 to .17 over, so one threshold means one thing; a one-second veto window buys the rules' safety at two thirds of the operator time, and a confidence gate lifts unflagged surprises from 12.5 % handled by rules to 75 %. Speed is part of the number: made to think for three seconds, the same judge drops from 29 to 18 of 30 because the body keeps moving. (3) The fleet can own the judgment: a 421M head distilled from the judge's probability vectors matches it (88.3 against 87.9 %) at 90 ms, and two rounds of the operator's vetoes, labelled by masked targets, give it the teacher's reading of unwritten situations (28 of 30) while it walks like the rules (95 % = 95 %), no cloud call in the loop; on the humanoid one round of vetoes takes the same head from 0 to 25 of 30 fresh unwritten situations, above the judge's 19 on those seeds, with no falls and no wrong hand-overs, and behind the veto window to 30 of 30. (4) The judge's recorded decisions compile into rules a person can read: filtered by the vetoes, the biped's draft handles 30 of 30 fresh runs, one more than the judge; on the humanoid a finer tree matches the judge at rule cost, and blind spots compile unless vetoed first. On 13,451 real recordings the judge triages trainability from motion facts alone (AUROC .78 against a hand rule's .64 and a fitted model's .88). Limits: the rules, the unwritten banks and their fixes have one author; ten episodes per situation; instruments were revised between rounds, each revision pre-registered and re-run for every arm.

## 1. Introduction
Robot fleets that are not yet autonomous run on a boundary: when does the policy act, and when does a person? For a teleoperation-first fleet that boundary sets the speed of the autonomy flywheel, and every handoff across it is a labelled record of where autonomy fails. Generative language models cannot sit there: too slow, too expensive per decision, and their confidence is not a probability. Hand-written rules cannot cover the long tail of situations. A new class of model — RLCD-trained System One models, of which TypeSafe's Jev is the first: typed, calibrated judgment over structured state, in ~100 ms for a fraction of a cent, unable to generate and unable to see — is a candidate for the boundary. Within a week of one such model's release, four independently built demonstrations (two driving simulators, a first-person game, a MuJoCo drone) and our own three systems converged on one architecture: code owns perception, arithmetic, safety and *when to ask*; the model owns categorical judgment; the probabilities are used as probabilities. This paper asks what that layer can and cannot do, and tests the answers across models rather than for one vendor.

Contributions: (1) a controlled closed-loop decomposition of "LLM in the loop helps" that separates the *option the model names* from the *judgment of when to use it*, with physical outcomes and paired-by-seed intervals; (2) abstention as a first-class output with its operator cost counted — a handoff operating curve (completion × operator-seconds × contact) against a geometric proxy and the planner's own logits; (3) state format as a necessary condition — no model tested read a raw number, and a calibrated judge read the same fact perfectly once code stated it categorically, while a small planner failed in both formats — so format decides whether a fact is seen and the model decides what it does with it; (4) vocabulary discovery from calibrated uncertainty on real operator text; (5) negative results under fair baselines and a methodological warning about program-solvable critic benchmarks.

## 3. The interface and how to present choices to it
The typed heads (choice / score / noul), parallel questions at one cost, calibrated probabilities. Design principles observed across seven systems and tested here: code owns arithmetic and safety; pre-digest state into relational or categorical facts; declare the agent's capabilities; factor decisions into heads; write options in the state's vocabulary; use probabilities as probabilities; judgments as commitments. One paragraph, one taxonomy figure; the four external systems are labelled unrefereed demonstrations. Field notes of 2026-09-18 (lab notebook) add thirteen systems and two substrate families — DOM or accessibility tables for software, state JSON for simulators and robots — and four further patterns: speculative fan-out of conditional heads executed only when the top-level choice makes them relevant (Browser Use); a closed-set rule for which turns a judgment head may answer at all, checkable from the tool schema (quackd); affordance-annotated options that carry code's predicted effect (dhul); and generation by selection over code-enumerated candidates, where the model chooses membership and order and code assembles the object (json-render, brush-stroke painting, camera paths). One reading of the interface is contested in the field and must be stated as a design rule: split probability over substitutable options is "several acceptable alternatives" (jev-browser, which deliberately has no low-confidence override), while over mutually exclusive options it is ambiguity or a missing category (ours); options must be exclusive if split mass is to be read as uncertainty. Four further moves were validated on the cell in one evening, each pre-registered: code owns the order of work and the waits while the model owns only the consequential destination (E75, E78b); a conflict between an instruction and a physical consequence is asked as two literal questions and combined in code, where a single Choice resolves toward the instruction (E76 vs E73); reading is bound to parts with a per-part Noul so reading errors become visible (E78b); and a note's conditions are extracted once into closed sets with code doing the matching and detecting ambiguity by counting matches (E79). The perception-limited ceiling is measured before arms are compared (E78b). The number→category effect of §6 is itself a vendor-documented caveat ("do the conversion in code and pass a named bucket"); what this paper adds is its measured size, its locus in the numeric fact rather than the prompt, and its model-class dependence.

## 4. Closed loop with the controller held fixed (H3, E68–E69)

**4.1 Instrument.** We fork a public MuJoCo quadrotor course (five stations: slalom, a low beam spanning the corridor, sweeping turnstiles, a sliding gate, a second beam and a pillar cluster; a rover to follow) and make three changes, each traceable to the adversarial review. One guidance loop serves every arm — the reactive layer, the commitment block, the action mapping, the risk slow-down and the 50 Hz safety reflex are shared — and only the *manoeuvre source* is pluggable: the published no-Jev baseline (no live source), a heuristic that steers to the wider side, the same heuristic with the repository's own climb predicate promoted to a rule, Jev, Jev with `climb` removed from its menu, and a replay source that serves recorded judgments by scene fingerprint at synthetic latency. The course is randomised by seed (pillar offsets, beam heights, turnstile phase, gate gap and phase, rover speed and amplitude); the seed decides the outcome deterministically for every arm without an API in the loop, so repeats are stability checks and the unit of analysis is the seed. Outcomes are physical: stations cleared, *clean* barrier crossing (no contact before the beam), contact-seconds and peak contact force. The perception front-end is the original's depth-and-segmentation Eye, checked against ground truth to 4 cm at 10 m. Analysis was fixed before any run: paired-by-seed bootstrap, Wilson intervals on proportions, no repeats counted as samples.

**4.2 The published claim, replicated.** As released, on the fixed course over 40 seeds, the no-Jev baseline crosses the beam on 12 % of seeds [5, 26] and cleanly on 5 % [1, 17], pinned in the reflex 73 % of the time with 0.15 s of contact and no crashes. The repository's "never past station 2" reproduces as 88 % of seeds. We also found that the published pair are two different controllers — the Jev branch commits to full-speed manoeuvres, the baseline's dodge is urgency-scaled and never sustained — so the published ablation confounds controller with judgment; §4.3 removes that confound, and we report both.

**4.3 Controlled comparison.** Randomised course, 40 seeds, controller held fixed (Table 1; Figure 2 per station). Clean barrier crossing rises from 10 % [4, 23] for the published baseline to 62 % [47, 76] when the heuristic gets the shared commitment block, to 80 % [65, 90] with the climb rule, to 98 % [87, 100] with Jev; median stations 1, 3, 4, 6. Paired by seed, sustained commitment alone is worth +53 points of clean crossing [+33, +70] and +1.6 stations, at +2.4 s of contact [+0.1, +5.9] and three crashes where the baseline had none — it walks along the 45 m beam and rounds its end; the climb rule is worth a further +17 [+3, +33] at no contact cost; Jev a further +17 [+5, +33] and +1.25 stations [+0.68, +1.85] over the climb rule, with contact not different (+0.34 s [−4.30, +4.53]). The arms separate at the sliding gate — 60 % of seeds pass it with the climb rule, 80 % with Jev — and diverge beyond it (second beam 28 % vs 78 %; cluster 28 % vs 60 %). Our pre-registered prediction that the climb rule would match Jev within 10 points failed in the model's favour. Removing `climb` from Jev's menu drops clean crossing to 38 % [24, 53] — 25 points *below* the heuristic [−45, −3] — because Jev's criteria brake at a fully blocked beam while the heuristic's blind slide rounds it; the option in Jev's hands is worth +60 points [+45, +75]. Judgment without the right vocabulary loses on progress to a heuristic with a mechanical trick; judgment with it clears the course. Ungated, Jev's failure mode is pinning against the sliding gate (five of forty episodes exceed 10 s of contact; median contact 0.20 s). Latency, replayed from recorded judgments: 0.10 s reproduces the live arm (−0.15 stations [−0.72, +0.40]); 0.30 s costs −0.47 [−0.93, −0.05]; 0.50 s −0.85 [−1.50, −0.25] with contact 6.5 s (Figure 3). Live median latency was 0.116 s at 88 calls per episode.

**4.4 The handoff head.** A gate over the calibrated outputs — risk ≥ τ, or target-lost ≥ 0.7 — hands the aircraft to an oracle operator (sees the true world; potential-field avoidance; 2 m/s; climbs over beams and arms; threads the gate) for 3 s, then returns control; operator time is counted. The same gate on the heuristic arms' geometric risk proxy is the fair counterpart. Gated on Jev's risk head at τ = 1.6, on 20 seeds paired with the ungated run, contact falls from 3.92 s to 0.21 s (−3.72 [−8.29, −0.04], −95 %), completion rises from 86 % to 95 %, and no episode exceeds 10 s of contact — the gate pinnings are absorbed — for 11.4 operator-seconds (18 % of the episode) in 3.9 handoffs, 95 % of them fired by the risk head and 83 % within 4 m of an obstacle. The geometric proxy, at any τ from 0.8 to 1.8, fires 5–8 times an episode for 15–22 operator-seconds with contact mostly *rising* and crashes rising from 2–3 to 6–14 per 40 — it fires when the nearest obstacle is already inside a metre. No arm has a point near five operator-seconds, so the pre-registered matched-time comparison could not be made; by a fallback declared before the Jev numbers existed, Jev's point Pareto-dominates every proxy point on operator time, contact and completion simultaneously (Figure 1). Predictions: contact −50 % → −95 %; completion unchanged or better → +9 points; operator time 5–15 % → 18 %, a miss. Gated at τ = 1.2 the eager edge appears: 38 % operator time, and the oracle operator itself pinned at the turnstiles in three episodes — a limit of our operator, not of the gate, and declared as such.

**4.5 What the closed loop shows and does not.** With the controller held fixed, most of the published gain is two categories the baseline could not express — sustained commitment and climb — and code can act on both once named. The judgment's residual value is timing and threading, at the stations a threshold cannot describe, and its abstention head is a better handoff trigger than a geometric proxy. It does not show a robot, a camera, or a human: one simulated airframe, sim-supplied perception, an oracle in place of an operator.

### 4.3 A second instrument: the sorting cell (E71)
A tabletop cell with six parts, colour and inspection trays, a person's hand that enters the corridor, blocked trays, slipping heavy parts, unknown objects, and one text-borne event per episode that the frozen rule policy was never written for (a relabel, a recall, a cosmetic override, a reroute, a precedence conflict, an ambiguous instruction), each in five wordings. Nine arms share one executor, one perception and one option enumerator. Over 40 seeds the judgment head sorts 87.1 % of parts correctly [82.2, 90.7] against 82.5 % for rules plus a keyword parser written from one wording and 79.2 % for the frozen rules (paired +4.6 [+1.7, +7.5] over the parser), reads the unanticipated notes 76 % of the time against 39 % and 21 %, and does so at the rules' operator time (7.7 % of the episode). Two things did not replicate from the drone. Confidence-gated handoff does not dominate the program's own abstention rule here: "ask whenever a note exists" reaches 93.3 % for 20 s of operator time, and at matched time the gate leaves more violations (.55 vs .40; AUROC of confidence against correctness .618), because probability mass spreads over substitutable options (which part to pick next) and because every surprise in this cell arrives flagged. And in the one time-critical exception, a hand appearing while a fragile heavy part is carried, the head obeys the literal pause instruction at confidence .53–.68, the gate converts that split into a four-second consultation, and the part slips and breaks during it: 0 of 6 for every Jev arm, 6 of 6 for an oracle that sets the part down first. Abstention is not free when the hazard is faster than the handoff. Pressing on both weaknesses moved one and explained the other. Read against the interpolated program line, the gate curves lie below it for operator budgets under about 12 s per episode and meet it at the flag rule's own operating point, so dominance is a statement about the fleet's operator budget, not a property of the head. Letting code choose which part to pick and asking the model only where it goes (E75) leaves accuracy unchanged and halves the operator cost of every gated ask, but lifts calibration only from .62 to .66: the residual errors are confident misreadings of the notes (an ambiguous instruction applied to the wrong part, a recall applied to the decoy lot, colour synonyms), and confidence flags option splits, not reading errors. Separating the destination into its own exclusive head (E72) raised decision-level calibration to .75 while costing 3.8 points of accuracy, because re-deciding after every grasp doubled the decisions. For the time-critical case, stating the slip consequence in the option text changed nothing (E73, 0 of 6), while the vendor's documented remedy for literal reading did: a parallel Noul "is it safe to freeze with what you hold?" answers .25 to .32 on every trial and, combined in code, saves the part 6 of 6 (E76). The judgment was available; the single-Choice format resolved the conflict toward the instruction. Assembling the fixes into one arm (code orders the parts and caps waits; the model answers three literal questions per decision — does the note change this part, where do covered parts go, is it safe to freeze — and one Choice for interrupts; code combines) reaches 90.0 % [85.6, 93.2] with zero broken parts at the rules' operator time (E78b; +3.3 [+1.2, +5.8] over the single-Choice arm), which is the ceiling a facts-only policy can reach in this cell: 21 of 240 parts carry uncorrected perception errors, so no policy without ground truth exceeds ≈ 91 %. The program's flag rule exceeds it, at 93 %, only because its asks buy the operator's ground truth about damage, and it pays six times the operator time for the three points. Reading the note once into closed-set conditions (colour, size, lot, shape, destination, singular reference) and letting code bind parts, count matches for ambiguity and apply the task's precedence rules brings the cell to 91.2 % [87.0, 94.2] with zero broken parts and zero judgment errors: of the 21 remaining misplacements, 19 are the eye's false alarms or missed damage and two are parts that bounced out of a tray (E79b; +4.2 [+1.7, +7.1] over the single-Choice arm). The wording of the extraction question decided the ambiguous case, 0 of 6 to 6 of 6, once it stated the exact condition. On 200 fresh seeds the design replicates: 89.3 % [87.5, 91.0] against a ceiling of 89.9 %, +5.3 [+3.6, +7.0] over the single-Choice arm on the same seeds, zero broken parts in 200 episodes, 92 % of the unanticipated notes handled, and 11 of 1,200 parts, under one percent, misplaced for any reason other than the eye or the physics (E82); the arm's weakest event type at that scale, the tray relabel at 20 of 27, owes all eight of its misses to damage false alarms on the affected parts, not to reading. The gain is the model's rather than the interface's: on 400 of the cell's recorded decisions a plain 7B open model behind the same typed questions and options chooses acceptably 37.5 % of the time against the calibrated model's 81.5 %, never pauses for the person's hand, and places 87 % of its answers on two letter positions regardless of content (D1-cell); whether a plain 72B does better is left to the GPU run. [figure 4: parts-correct with intervals; figure 5: the (violations, operator-seconds) plane with both gate curves and the program line; the precedence timeline]

A third autonomy level sits between act and ask (E88): below the confidence threshold the robot announces its intended action and holds still for one second while the operator may veto; a veto costs a full ask. On both banks and at thresholds .6–.8 this confirm level matches the ask gate's violations (paired −.03 [−.15, +.10] per episode at τ = .7, unflagged bank) at 63–66 % of its operator time (−7.4 s [−9.0, −5.8] per episode), degrades to +.10 [+.03, +.20] violations when the operator misses one wrong proposal in four, and is bounded by the model's own error rate below the threshold: 27 % of proposals were vetoed, and veto time is the time it cannot save. It does not save the time-critical precedence case (0 of 6, as the ask); that case needed the split question (E76), not a shorter wait.

## 5. The perception→judgment seam (H5/A1)

We corrupt what the judge reads — not what the reflex reads — the way perception fails: correlated range error, target dropout in runs of frames, phantom obstacles, stale frames. On the deterministic climb-rule arm the curve is flat to improving, because a threshold judge's corrupted proposals are merely randomised while the clean reflex keeps it safe, and only fully stale frames hurt (68 → 58 % completion); n = 20 per cell and none of it clears noise, and nominal corruption rate is not a clean axis (run-based channels corrupt 72 % of frames at rate .3), so we report the measured fraction. With Jev in the loop at rate .6 — 90–100 % of its frames corrupted — completion moves by −0.05 to −0.40 stations against the same seeds uncorrupted, every interval spanning zero; clean crossing stays 80–100 %. Our pre-registered prediction of roughly linear degradation was wrong: a judgment consulted as a commitment at a few decisive moments, with safety owned by code on the clean scene, tolerates substantial noise on its own inputs. Adding per-fact confidence fields did not help (completion slightly lower, contact mixed, wide intervals); we note, as a hypothesis formed after the fact, that those fields were numbers, and §6.3 says numbers are close to invisible to this judge — a categorical form is the natural next experiment. Corrupting the reflex's inputs as well would measure the controller, not the judge, and is left to future work. [Per-frame risk was not logged; the "fails confidently" half of the prediction is untested.]

On real wrist-camera frames (the 48 E64 tiles) the seam is region-limited before it is judgment-limited. A text-promptable segmenter (SAM 3) returns nothing for any "gripper" phrasing with either its distilled or its official text encoder, and boxes the claw in 88 % of frames when it is named by appearance ("black robotic claw"): detectors ground nouns as visual concepts, so the eye must name what a thing looks like, and that translation is code's. With the claw boxed, a one-line geometric feature separates place from drop one second after release at AUROC .79 [.65, .92] (73 % leave-one-out, 48 tiles, post hoc detector threshold) — the first signal past the blind-human line — but the box it measures is the manipulated object in three of eight inspected frames, so it is recorded as a lead. An open relation model given the same regions does not read "holding" for a gripper (.58); given exact regions on the rendered cell it reads containment at .995 while two geometric features reach .997. The missing piece on real frames is the identity of the manipulated object, not a better relation: tracking it from the release frame, where it is inside the claw by construction, is the next instrument.

## 6. Text probes with fair baselines (H1, E65–E66, E59–E63, E70)

**6.1 A critic benchmark built from plan templates is a program.** Our first critic suite — 400 four-way "which step next" questions over real DROID instructions, distractors typed as skip-ahead, repeat, wrong object, irrelevant and post-failure — read as a strong result for the judgment model (96.8 % vs 85.8 % for a 7B's letter logits, lexical overlap 49 %). An adversarial reviewer then wrote a twelve-line canonical-plan tracker that scores 100 %; our reimplementation scores 99.5 % on that suite and 75 % on the harder tier (Jev 60 %). The lexical baseline that was supposed to falsify "word-matching, not state-tracking" never read the completed-steps field the generator used, so it could not fire. We report the program as an arm on every suite where a program can be written; a suite a program solves is a probe of the program.

**6.2 Readouts decide calibration claims.** Against the same 7B, three readouts give three different models. The multiple-choice letter readout: 85.8 % accuracy, ECE .12, AUROC .81, confidence .92 when wrong. Constrained length-normalised scoring of the candidate *strings*, demanded as the fairer interface: 75.0 % on the base tier and 36.9 % on the hard tier with a clean bullet prompt (ECE .063, AUROC .816 on the base tier) — ten points behind the letter readout on accuracy and better calibrated raw. A first version of this arm scored 64.5 % and 11.9 % because our prompt listed candidates as "A. text" while scoring the bare text, losing the mass on the letter-prefixed form; a re-check declared before the numbers were trusted caught it (method error 18). Per-candidate yes/no heads at Jev's call budget: AUROC .891 on the base tier against Jev's `noul` at .989, and .589 on the hard tier. A single temperature fitted on a held-out task split moves the letter readout's ECE from .136 to .051 and its confidence-when-wrong from .92 to .75, and under that scalar the ambiguity result *inverts* (7B .43 vs Jev .71 on pairs with two acceptable answers). What survives fair readouts is the ranking gap — AUROC .97 vs ~.78 — and the gate gap on the base tier; what does not is most of the ECE gap and the ambiguity claim as first stated. [72B arms: GPU run 1, pending.]

**6.3 State format decides whether a fact is seen; the model decides what it does with it.** The same fact presented two ways, truth by construction, 160 base items paired: after a grasp, "gripper force sensor reads 0.0 N" versus "gripper reports: nothing held". Jev retries on 6.2 % of the numeric items and 100 % of the categorical ones (+46.9 points [+39.4, +54.4]); on benign items it continues in 100 % of both formats — the number is ignored, not misread. In the dispatch setting (load against a threshold rule, raw versus code-evaluated) 66.7 % → 100 % (+33 [+13, +60]). The program arm scores 100 % in every cell, which is the point: where a number is the input, a program owns it, and the model's job begins where the program's ends. The 7B's letter readout, by contrast, gains only +5.6 points [+2.5, +9.4] from the categorical format — 0 % numeric, 11 % categorical — because it never retries in either: told "nothing held", it lifts 89 % of the time. Our pre-registered prediction that every model would gain at least 15 points fails for the 7B. The categorical fact is necessary for a judge to act on a fact and not sufficient; whether a larger open model behaves like the judge or like the small planner is the 72B run's question.

**6.4 Irreducible items.** Seventy-five situations in five categories (intent, staleness, rule conflict in context, hedged reports, social deference) were written with a program rule frozen *before* any model output; the rule solves 23, leaving 52 whose answer turns on a soft cue. Ground truth is an independent rater's majority [pending]. Provisionally, against the author's own sets — a stand-in, not ground truth — Jev's action falls inside the acceptable set on 79 % of the irreducible items and the 7B's on 73 % (the program's 0 % is by construction); on the rule-solvable items Jev 87 %, 7B 74 %, program 100 %. The two abstention heads err in opposite directions: Jev's "ask a human" fires on 100 % of the items the author marked ask-human and on 62 % of the rest; the 7B's on 70 % and 35 %. As phrased, Jev's head abstains far too often — for a handoff trigger that is operator time spent on decidable situations — and its threshold is a knob the rater's set will place. Jev and the 7B choose the same action on 52 % of items. [Rater pending; every number in this paragraph is provisional.]

**6.5 Vocabulary from uncertainty.** On 1,233 real disengagement reports through a nine-way cause menu, 6.8 % of reports split (no option above .5) and 1.9 % chose "unclear". Three categories proposed from half the splits — anticipatory takeover, expectation mismatch, ODD exit — resolved 21 of 42 held-out splits (re-asking the original menu: 1 of 42) and left 173 of 200 clear reports unchanged, 24 of the 27 changes being absorption into the new categories. On six fresh wordings of a recurring fault the same mechanism took a categorical head from 2/12 to 12/12 with 0/10 of a resolved-with-history control disturbed. [Menu-size null and cluster-bootstrap intervals pending.] Split rate is a property of the writer — formulaic reports 0 %, narrative reports 16–21 %.

## 7. Negative results
Expected-cost dispatch from calibrated probabilities ties a constant always-autonomous policy on the only real fleet data we have (E53). One veto→reason→re-plan round adds nothing with a 7B planner: 54.7 % → 54.0 % acceptable steps, with 2 false vetoes in 82 and the right reasons given — the planner answered "you ignored the reported failure" with "inspect the gripper" 36 times in 37 (E67). Perception is the wall: on 48 wrist-camera frames a blind human scored 45 % at place-vs-drop and a 7B VLM was degenerate (E64). Raw numbers are not the model's input (E55, E65b, E70). An in-sample veto figure of .984, produced by criteria revised after seeing the model's outputs, is retracted in favour of the out-of-sample .778 (method error 16). Judgment without the option it needs loses to a heuristic with a mechanical trick (E68, `jev_noclimb`). In the sorting cell the confidence gate does not dominate a program's flag-based abstention rule at that rule's operator budget, and no abstention helps in the time-critical precedence case (E71, claims 4.27–4.28); stating the physical consequence in the option text does not change the choice either (E73, 0 of 6), and the calibration of a single action head tops out near .66 because its residual errors are confident misreadings of notes rather than option splits (E75). Two external results, from benches unrelated to ours, converge on the same mechanisms: a local option-scoring parking head keeps 86 % per-step agreement with its teacher yet parks one time in five once sensors replace an oracle error vector, and its entropy confidence stops separating good from bad decisions — agreement flat at .30 across every confidence bucket (ochotzas, 2026-09-18); a direct Jev judge on a hard cue-aggregation task reports confidence ≥ .9 on 42 % of rows and is 72 % accurate on exactly those, while a character-bigram naive Bayes ties the best decomposed pipeline at zero API cost (ikkun, 2026-09-17).

Later additions. The RLCD training recipe adds nothing over plain soft distillation for a 421M owned head at 4,252 records: −4.6 points in the loop and −3.8 in agreement with the teacher (E91). A one-second confirm window does not save the precedence case either (0 of 6, E88). Jev's chosen action depends on the order the options are listed in for 16 % of recorded decisions against 5.5 % for a same-order repeat, entirely below confidence .7 (50 % of argmaxes flip below .5, 1.7 % in [.7, .9), none at ≥ .9; D5), and averaging the probabilities over four random orders recovers nothing there (+0.0 points [−3.0, +3.0] in [.5, .7); D5b): the order-fragile decisions are uncertain, not mis-ordered, and belong to the gate. On the perception seam, a distilled-text-encoder SAM 3 finds no "gripper" in 96 real wrist frames while finding the claw under "robot arm" (E85, E85b); masks with hand-written geometry do not separate place from drop on the E64 tiles under any of three evaluations (E85c); an open relation model reads "part inside tray" from exact regions at .995 while a two-feature geometric rule reaches .997 and the model adds +.011 [+.002, +.028] on the cases boxes cannot resolve (E86, E86b); and neither the rendered nor the real frames yield a readable "holding" for a gripper (E86 .666 where boxes overlap; E87 .565 release-vs-open). The hard code-plus-model gate of E86b lost information on saturated containment (method note) — a soft combiner is the right composition.

## 8. Discussion and limits

**What a fleet gets.** At the boundary between policy and person, the measured value of a typed judgment interface is narrow and specific: it names categories the code lacked and times their use (climb at the beam, threading at the gate, recurring fault, ODD exit), and its calibrated abstention is a better handoff trigger than a geometric proxy — 95 % less contact for 18 % operator time, dominating the proxy on every axis. Everything computable — numbers against thresholds, units, geometry, safety, when to ask — belongs to code, and the four-times-replicated state-format result says the model does not merely do those worse, it does not see them. The design that follows is the one the field's first-week builders converged on independently: code prepares categorical facts and owns the veto; the model chooses among options written in the facts' vocabulary; the probabilities are used as probabilities.

**What the model is not.** It is not a perception system (E64), not a planner-repair loop with a small planner (E67), not a better dispatcher than a constant policy on the real fleet data we had (E53), and not a critic of anything a program can already decide (E65). Its calibration advantage over a small open model survives as a ranking gap and largely not as an ECE gap once the baseline is temperature-scaled; the ambiguity claim inverted under a fair readout. In the manipulation domain the attribution line on 400 recorded decisions runs from chance to parity: two small open models read through a Jev-shaped label-logit interface choose acceptably 22–27 % of the time, a plain 7B with a letter readout 37.5 %, a 35B mixture with 3 B active 73.5 %, and a dense open 27B 84.8 % against Jev's 81.5 % (paired +3.2, [−0.5, +7.0]) (D1-cell, D4, D4c). What the calibrated-decision model keeps is not the argmax and, on records, not calibration either: on the 126 of those decisions with a single acceptable action the 27B's confidence is as reliable as Jev's (ECE .028 against .070, AUROC .88 against .82; D7, correcting an earlier comparison that carried a subset figure — method error 23). What it keeps is calibration *in the loop*: when each model steers into its own states, Jev's confidence stays honest (over-confidence −.03 to −.07, ECE .08–.10 on two banks) and the 27B's turns over-confident (+.135, ECE .15; its single-answer accuracy falls from 88 to 72 %), which is the −9.2 points ungated of D4d; the 27B's ranking survives (AUROC .90 in its own states), which is why a .7 gate recovers all but 1.3 points at the price of asks. A second RLCD checkpoint from the same API (`jev-preview`) reproduces the profile behind the identical request: 95 % agreement with the first on recorded decisions, over-confidence −.06 in its own states, the identical 30 of 40 surprises handled when gated (D8) — so the property belongs at least to the family, and the boundary is that both checkpoints come from one vendor. Figure 8 draws the three reliability diagrams (recorded decisions with one right answer; the held-out teacher against the owned head; each model's own states in the loop, the owned head among them). It also keeps order stability — 16 % of its answers move when the options are reordered against the 27B's 32 %, none of its confident answers where one in five of the 27B's do (D5, D4) — and it answers in 0.13 s at $0.04 per million tokens. The interface is commodity and a large enough general model matches the choice; what the judgment layer keeps is a confidence that means the same thing on its own states as on the teacher's, and that is what a fixed threshold in the operating plane spends (D4d, D7). The letter-position bias of the small plain model appears in Jev only where its confidence is below .7 (D5): attribution by confidence band, not by a single number.

**The design that follows.** Code owns the order of work, the waits, the default rule, the safety combination and the arithmetic; the model answers narrow literal questions in one parallel call — does this note change this part, where do covered parts go, is it safe to freeze with what is held — and code combines them. In the cell that design reaches the perception-limited ceiling with zero broken parts at the rules' operator time (E78b). Its failure mode is the code author's: a wait without a cap, an order without a skip rule, reproduce the rule policy's failures inside the judgment arm, and only the decision logs show it.

**What a fleet keeps when it owns the head (E77).** A 527-thousand-parameter option-attention student distilled from 3.8 thousand recorded Jev decisions agrees with the teacher on 85 % of held-out records, answers in 10 ms on a laptop, and sorts 74 % of parts correctly on the seeds it was trained on but 59 % on new seeds where the teacher sorts 88 %. The failure is a single mode: the student pauses when no hand is present (615 of 887 held-out decisions), a frequent, cheap option in the teacher's records that the student stopped conditioning on the hand flag; each spurious pause produces a state the teacher never visited and the episode drifts. Record-level agreement did not predict closed-loop competence, the same lesson jev-park reported from the other direction, and the remedy is the data loop itself: one round in which the teacher labels the 834 states the student visited lifts it to 75.8 % [70.0, 80.8] on the held-out seeds, +16.7 [+6.7, +27.5], and removes the collapse (615 spurious pauses to 10), leaving a gap of 12 points to the teacher that is reading — the student still cannot follow a relabel note in any of its five wordings — and over-asking (E80). Twice the teacher's own data, from 200 new episodes, does not do this: the collapse stays (63.7 %, 541 spurious pauses), twelve points below the corrected student on the same seeds (E81). Sovereignty is not a checkpoint, it is that correction loop running, and the head's size is the ceiling it runs into. A 421-million-parameter pretrained typed-decision encoder lifts that ceiling (E90–E91): distilled from 4,252 records it drives held-out seeds at 79.6 % with its native RLCD recipe and at 84.2 % [79.0, 88.2] with plain soft cross-entropy on the teacher's probability vector (+4.6 [+1.7, +8.3] paired); with 6,489 records the plainly distilled head reaches 88.3 % [83.7, 91.8], paired +0.4 [−0.8, +1.7] against the teacher's own 87.9 %, with the teacher's decisions per episode, pauses and per-event profile, at 90 ms on-device (E91c); the recipe head at 2× gives 83.3 %; with the governor's set-down rule (E92) the 2× head reaches 90.4 %, level with the teacher under the same rule (paired +0.0 [−1.3, +1.3]) and 2.5 points above the teacher as originally deployed, with broken parts at zero; the precedence note stays unread by every head (0 of 7). Two things travel in the record and one does not. A head trained on the teacher's chosen action alone is over-confident (single-answer top-1 probability .95 against a hit rate of .89) and uninformative in ranking (AUROC .56 against .68–.76), and drives worst (77.9 %, 8.65 pauses per episode): the ranking a governor spends is in the probability vector, and a label log discards it. The training recipe is not required to inherit it — with soft targets the proper-scoring optimum and the cross-entropy optimum coincide and the recipe's sampled noise is variance. On the top-1 probability the soft-target heads are calibrated within ±.06 on held-out single-answer decisions, the 2× head within .015 against the teacher's .01 (E91c); the head's reported confidence is a normalised-entropy score that runs ~.15 lower and must not be gated at a probability threshold (method error 27). The dial survives the shift into the head's own states, which is where a governor spends it: driving held-out seeds itself, the 2× plainly distilled head reports over −.007 and ECE .054 on 127 single-answer decisions, against the cloud teacher's +.014 / .083 on the same bank and the open 27B's +.135 / .153 (D7b, four of four predictions pre-registered before scoring). What plain distillation transfers is therefore not only the ranking but the calibration, at 90 ms on-device with no call leaving the robot. Boundary: n = 127 in one instrument with one teacher, no interval computed on that ECE, and the head's *states* shift while the *world* does not — domain shift is untested here. Figure 9 draws the ladder from the byte student to the teacher with the governor's set-down rule, beside the attribution dumbbells (recorded decisions against the loop) for every judge.

**Teacher, not only service.** Gundala's open 1B reproduction of the typed-parallel interface at 150 ms on a laptop suggests the interface is a pattern any model can wear; the calibrated cloud model's durable role may be as the teacher and arbiter of last resort for on-device copies, and the vocabulary loop is how a fleet would build the categories those copies judge over — from its own notes, without a hand-written taxonomy.

**Limits.** One simulated airframe on one course family; sim-supplied perception and an oracle in place of an operator; two checkpoints of one vendor's RLCD family (`jev-latest`, `jev-preview`; D8), dated per run, with ±0.08 run-to-run drift in probabilities; author-written probes, with independent-rater ground truth still pending for the irreducible items; n = 20 per gate threshold; a 7B, not a frontier planner, as the baseline in most text probes. As of 2026-09-18 we found no other closed-loop robotics comparison of a judgment head against a baseline controller with seeds and intervals: the field's robotics uses (a real SO-101 arm behind a multi-robot CLI whose own documentation says nobody has measured the head on a robot, two simulated arms with one video each, a rigorous but 0.5B local parking head) are demonstrations or self-declared estimates, and a public request for a measured vouch (Kinsley, 2026-09-18) drew theory and no numbers. The method appendix lists twenty-five errors we caught in our own work along the way, three of them in claims that had already been stated in earlier drafts and three in the instrument built on the last night; we consider that list part of the result.

### 8.x Why a calibrated decision model in the seat, and not a frontier model; what to test next

The seat has four requirements — a decision every 0.1–1 s, a probability that keeps its meaning on the model's own states, a per-decision cost a fleet can afford at 10^5 decisions per robot-day, and a model the fleet can own and retrain — and a frontier vision-language model meets one. We did not run a frontier model in the seat; the nearest affordable test, a dense open 27B behind the identical interface, kept the judge's accuracy and drifted its number by .135 in its own states (D4, D7), and injected think time of 3 s cost the calibrated judge 11 of 29 unwritten situations and doubled near-contacts on a moving body (E105). Frontier models belong outside the loop: constructing the reflex, translating the world once, teaching. The untested items, singly and in combination, are: a frontier model in the seat at its own latency and as the copy's teacher; a human-sized body in a home room and a store aisle (an open G1 walking policy runs headless in our harness); one operator for many robots; real takeover logs as the correction source and shadow scoring of the teleop stream; cadence without staleness; a site twin as the bench's world; the annotator on the remaining real recordings; and the one unsolved anticipated situation, a person crossing the path, as a governor rule.

A second System One model answered part of this. CLM-8B, open, with Jev's typed interface and a contrastive lookup
inside (a frozen encoder, two projection heads, a softmax over scaled cosines), run zero-shot on the station's own decisions,
chose an acceptable action 12 % of the time at a mean stated probability of .58, an expected calibration error of .50 against
Jev's .02 on the same kind of decisions, and handled the unwritten lines only by handing every one to the operator. The
interface does not make the number; the model does. "Calibrated decision model" is a claim about the probability, to be
measured for each model that makes it, and post-training on the fleet's own records is the test of whether a cheaper
architecture can earn it. It can, on the distribution the records cover: post-trained for eight seconds on the same records as
the generative copy, the heads handle every unwritten line at no operator cost with an expected calibration error of .06,
and miss the finer endings the copy learned from identical records (35 against 60 on a second bank). The extra capacity
buys resolution where the finer note lives, and the veto window buys it back at nineteen operator seconds a line.
On the humanoid the same recipe takes the open model from seven to twenty-six of thirty and from none to thirty of thirty
with no wrong hand-over, and leaves its number uncalibrated (an expected calibration error of .25) and its gait undecided,
where the generative copy is whole and sure: the cheap architecture learns what to do from the fleet's records on both
benches and learns how sure to be only where decisions follow from facts rather than from geometry and time.


### 8.y The rules written a second time (E114)

The obvious objection to every "where no rule was written" number is that a rule can always be written. We measured it: the
rule programs' author, given the unseen banks and the rooms' acceptable sets, rewrote both programs. On the duck the rewritten
rules handle 20 of 30 at the first attempt (six lines, nine minutes) and 29 of 30 after one more line informed by one trace, the
judge's number; on the humanoid 30 of 30 (eight lines, nine minutes) against the judge's 20. The anticipated banks were
unchanged (95 % = 95 %; 39 of 40 = 39 of 40). So the calibrated judge's value on unwritten situations is the interval between a
situation's first appearance and a programmer's fix, plus a number a governor can spend during that interval; it is not
accuracy after the fact. Two caveats are the banks' author wrote the fixes (a lower bound on a stranger's minutes, an upper
bound on a stranger's program), and the first attempt exposed an instrument flaw shared by every arm (the doorway fact fires
inside the kick zone; method error 36), which a deterministic program finds and a stochastic judge crosses by luck.

### 8.z The judge's decisions as a rule draft (E115, E115b)

If the judge's value is the interval before a rule exists, its recorded decisions in that interval should shorten it. We
compiled them into a decision tree over the same categorical facts the rule programs read and placed it in front of the frozen
program. Filtered to the decisions the operator would not have vetoed, the duck's draft (eleven leaves; "if the note says
follow, follow; if standing and the person has crutches, wait") handled 30 of 30 fresh runs of the unseen situations, one more
than the judge, and scoped by a novelty gate to states outside the anticipated bank's vocabulary it left that bank at the frozen
rules' 36 of 40. On the humanoid a third of the decisions survive the veto and the filtered draft fails; a finer tree from all
decisions matches the judge (20 of 30) at rule cost and inherits its blind spot. The procedure that follows is judge, veto,
compile, scope, review; the review is where the tree is silent. Six pre-registered predictions went one of six and the
follow-up's four went three of four; the misses are the instrument flaw of §8.y and the compiler's capacity.

### 8.w A fourth bench: the picking station at decision level (E117–E123)

The loop a warehouse or store picker runs on, a grasp score, a threshold and a remote person for the rest, was modelled at
decision level with no physics: a seeded grasp scorer, a verify check, stated durations, and a picker whose answer costs twenty
seconds and resolves what the robot could not. The same shape as the other three benches appeared with two sharp edges. On
sixty unwritten lines, each announced by a note, the frozen rules shipped every item and the judge shipped none, reading two
of three notes completely and turning the recalled lot into a flagged exception; on forty written lines the rules handled all
forty and the judge 32, six of its misses being double picks placed at a stated .73 to .95, the one error class no threshold
catches. As the mix of lines hardened the gated judge escalated more and lost fewer lines than the rules, but escalated a fifth
of clean lines for nothing. The rules rewritten by their author after reading the lines handled every line in three clauses
and two minutes, and the judge's own decisions with the vetoed ones removed compiled into a three-leaf rule that did the same
at no operator cost. The owned copy transferred whole and, after one masked correction round, handled every fresh unwritten
line with nothing shipped wrong, at fifteen operator seconds per line: the veto had taught which action was wrong, not which
acceptable action was cheapest, and the copy chose to ask. Labelling the same visited states with the operator's replacement
instead, the cheapest acceptable action at each, gave a copy that handled every fresh unwritten line with nothing shipped
wrong, no asks and no operator time at the rules' speed, beating the judge that taught it (40 of 60 at seven operator
seconds) and tying the hindsight programmer and the drafted rule: the veto says not that, the replacement says this instead,
and a fleet's takeover logs carry the second for free. In simulation the replacement is the oracle's action; in a fleet it
is what the operator did.
The copy's one written-bank blind spot is its teacher's, the double pick: at states that say holding two items and heavier
than expected it places the pair at a stated .58 to .92, through a correction round and the same round weighted four
times; forty more written lines of takeovers, thirty-three put-back records instead of twenty-four, take it to nine of
ten, though its number on the pattern is inverted, .39 to .64 where it puts back and .75 to .89 where it still places
the pair. A one-clause rule reads the fact ten of ten in a minute with no residue. The division of labour this bench ends
on is the rule for what the sensor says, the judge for what the note says, the copy for what the corrections covered, with
the price of "covered" measured in lines.
A second unwritten bank, designed after the copy's corrections and carrying no new fact keys or options, replicates the
humanoid's story at decision level: the copies ship a mismatched label and a held lot thirty-nine to forty of forty at a
stated .80 to .87, the judge handles all forty from the note, one replacement round on thirty lines makes the copy whole with
its old lines kept, and the copy's training vocabulary flags every new-bank decision and none of the old. Two twists
belong in the record: the copies handle crushed packaging twenty of twenty with no note read, because the earlier leak
correction had taught them that a condition other than dry goes to the return bin (blindness is about facts, not banks);
and the judge reads "must not ship" and skips every damaged line, nothing shipped and nothing returned.

### 8.v The world changing on its own, and two noise floors (E126–E132)

Three situations on the humanoid arise after the task has started: the cup starts leaking in the robot's hand, the
requester walks off as the robot reaches her, a second person asks for the cup. Each carries the note an operator would
leave. The frozen rules and the rules rewritten with hindsight for the previous unwritten bank cannot see any of the three
and hand the cup over wrongly thirty times out of thirty; the judge, reading the day's note, handles all thirty with no
wrong hand-over and no fall. Ten of its episodes ran to the clock, and the reason turned out to be the bench's: the
departing requester was walked to the far corner of the room and stopped there, so the facts said in the room, standing
still, while the story said she had left, and the judge kept walking toward her with done rated at zero over fifteen
hundred decisions. Rewording the done option changed nothing (four of five predictions, the one that mattered failed);
letting her leave and saying so in the facts had the judge put the cup down, tell the operator and finish, ten of ten (five
of five). The lesson is written as a method error: a situation's facts must tell the story its note and its acceptable set
assume, or the judge's failure is the bench's.

The owned copies were run on the same bank after their corrections on the earlier one, the nearest thing to a second
designer's bank we could make ourselves. They cannot read the new notes: each hands the leaking cup over and hands to the
departing requester twenty times out of twenty, passing the third situation by habit. Worse, each correction round had
raised the copy's stated confidence on situations it had never seen, from .82 to .94 at the fatal hand-over, so the veto
window's rescue shrank from 29 of 30 with the uncorrected copy to 10 of 30 with the twice-corrected one. This is the
clearest statement in the report of why a calibrated model sits in the seat: the judge is the out-of-distribution reader,
the copy the in-distribution owner, and correction erodes the calibration that separates them. (Post-training the body
itself, the humanoid's exported walking policy fine-tuned by PPO on the decision layer's command stream, fixes the fault it
is given, standing speed .357 to .115 m/s with no fall in a hundred episodes, and breaks the code written around the fault:
the bench's −0.2 stand command, a patch for the old body's creep, walks the new body backwards and the judge falls from
20–23 to 10 of 30 until the patch is removed, after which the rules are whole for the first time. A bounded edit over the
frozen policy gets two thirds of the improvement without a fall in training and inside the old contracts. Perturbing every mass, friction and actuator gain by up to twenty
percent per episode leaves all three walkers' outcomes intact and thirty percent breaks the shipped policy and the fine-tune
but not the bounded edit: the tolerance a simulator must meet on this body is about twenty percent on those parameters,
and post-training on one parameter set did not narrow it.) Removing the patch fixed the contract it had broken and exposed two
more that the shipped body's creep had been meeting without code, the judge's out-of-reach pick-up, which the creep turned into a success by drifting the body
in, and the judge's waiting distance against the requester's noticing radius: a body's fault had become load-bearing,
and post-training the fault away exposed every place the code leaned on it.
Fixing the pick-up's contract restored the fine-tuned body (departures four to ten of ten, the written bank thirty-eight of
forty) and exposed two more: a leak timed from the pick-up that a fast approach could not see before handing over, and
scripted people and events timed by the clock. A bench for a decision layer triggers its events on the robot's state and
scores each decision on what the robot saw, or the body's speed is a hidden factor in every number.
On that bench, five revisions later, post-training the body makes the frozen rules whole (forty of forty on the fine-tuned
body) and costs the judge (thirty-seven to thirty on the written bank, twenty-one to eleven on the unwritten): its habits,
learned on a body that crept, meet people and wait too far on a body that stands still. The rules were written against
geometry and survive a change of body; the judge's geometry was learned from the old body's behaviour, and a fleet that
post-trains its policy re-corrects its decision layer on the new body.
A cascade on the copy's number does not repair this: read off the same records, a line that routes the uncorrected copy's
twenty fatal decisions to the judge sits at .85 and sends nine decisions in ten, and no line below one catches all of the
twice-corrected copy's. The detector of "outside the corrections" has to come from somewhere other than the copy.
With a second architecture whose rounds cost seconds the whole curve is measurable, and it is two curves in opposite
directions from the same data: six cumulative rounds of the fleet's own takeovers drive the expected calibration error from
.362 to .008 on the lines they cover and from .307 to .399 on a bank they never touch, the stated probability at the wrong
decisions there climbing from .62 to .90, while the operator's veto window falls from rescuing thirty-four lines of sixty to
rescuing none. The round that finally covers that bank restores all three. A falling intervention rate is therefore not
evidence of a safer fleet unless an untouched bank is scored every round, and that measurement costs seconds.
One replacement-label round on the new bank's own takeovers then makes the copy whole: thirty of thirty on fresh seeds of the
new bank with no wrong hand-over and no operator time, and thirty of thirty on the old bank, where the round also took the
reaching child from five to ten with no zone entry, above the oracle and the judge; its stated confidence on the old bank
fell rather than rose, so the label form decides how sure the copy becomes as well as what it learns. The loop closes one
bank behind the world; what stands between the banks is the judge, or a gate that knows the situation is new. That gate exists and is not a line
on the copy's number: a decision whose facts, note words or options contain a feature outside the copy's training
vocabulary goes to the judge. Behind it the copy handles the new bank thirty of thirty with every decision routed and its
own bank at its own score with none routed, no false alarm in twelve hundred decisions. With the notes hidden the judge
still handles the new bank thirty of thirty from the facts alone, so on this bank the note bought speed, not correctness.

The same week produced the noise floors. The API is not bit-reproducible: on identical states the stated probability
differs between runs by 0.003 on average and up to 0.13 on one option. On the humanoid a close call sends the physics down a
different path, and five runs of the same judge on the same thirty seeds gave 20, 21, 21, 22, 23, so an earlier "best
score" of 22 against 20 was withdrawn as noise while the structural change it came with, ten livelocked episodes to none,
stands. On the station, decision-level and without physics, three runs gave identical outcomes to the line. Every ladder in
this report now carries its floor: about two on the humanoid, zero on the station.
